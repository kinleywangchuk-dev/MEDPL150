Readings: Reflection Journal

Reading 1 — Susan Sontag, “In Plato’s Cave” (On Photography)

Sontag’s opening chapter makes me think about photography as more than memory-keeping. She describes photographs as a “grammar and…an ethics of seeing,” and that hit me because it suggests every photo is a decision about what matters. I’m used to taking pictures quickly—screenshots, selfies, random moments with friends, or something I think looks cool. But Sontag frames image-taking as a way of collecting and organizing the world, almost like building an “anthology of images” in your head. That makes me realize that my camera isn’t neutral. The moment I pull it out, I’m choosing what gets attention, what gets saved, and what becomes “real” later because I can point to an image as proof.

One idea that stood out is Sontag’s claim that photographing can feel like knowledge and power. A photo can make something seem undeniable—“it happened”—even though it can still distort, crop, or stage reality. I notice this a lot online: the same event can look completely different depending on who took the photo and what angle they used. When I take photos, I’m not only documenting; I’m framing. I might crop out a messy background, wait for better lighting, or choose an angle that makes something look more dramatic. Sontag argues that even when we think photography is “just capturing,” it’s still interpretation, shaped by taste and intention. That makes me want to be more honest with myself about why I’m choosing certain images and what story they create.

Sontag also talks about photography as both participation and non-intervention. The line that “the person who is recording cannot intervene” is uncomfortable but real—especially today when people film everything. Sometimes the camera helps expose injustice, but sometimes filming becomes a way to distance yourself from what’s happening. I don’t think most people are trying to be cruel, but the habit of turning moments into content can make emotions feel flatter, like we’re collecting “proof” instead of fully living the experience. I recognize that in myself too. On trips or at important moments, I can get so focused on getting a “good” photo that I’m half outside the moment.

Thinking about how to represent “our world at this moment,” I keep coming back to the feeling Sontag describes: we live in an image-choked world, where everything seems to exist to end in a photograph. Visually, I think our world right now could be represented through contrasts: crowded city life but people isolated in headphones; “perfect” social media images versus the exhaustion behind them; constant news imagery that shocks us for a second and then disappears into the feed. If I were to represent this through photography and graphics, I’d use a mix of documentary-style photos and text overlays. The photo would show the surface (commute, storefronts, screens, advertisements), and the text could expose what’s beneath (pressure, anxiety, identity, hope). I could also use cropping intentionally to show how framing changes meaning—one image that looks normal, and a second crop that reveals something hidden.

Finally, the idea of building nine projects as a body of work helps me see the semester as a developing point of view, not just separate assignments. Each project can teach me a tool, but also a way of seeing: how I frame, what I choose to show, and how I combine image and text to communicate. If photography is an “ethics of seeing,” then my job as a media maker is to practice seeing more responsibly—more aware of what I’m turning into an image, why I’m doing it, and what impact it might have on the viewer.
