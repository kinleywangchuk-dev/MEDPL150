Readings: Reflection Journal

Reading 3 — Motherhood in Crisis: A Literary Analysis


In Motherhood in Crisis by Laurence Ivil, Alicia Prager, and Saidu Bah, four women from Sierra Leone share their experiences with pregnancy and motherhood in a country where healthcare is severely limited. The graphic novel format makes these stories especially powerful, seeing the emotions on the women's faces creates a sense of closeness to experiences that might otherwise feel distant. Each story is different, but they all point to the same underlying argument: the dangers these women face are not inevitable, they are the result of systemic failures in healthcare, gender equality, and social support.


The first two stories focus on healthcare access and gender inequality. Memunatu suffers from unexplained bleeding after a stillbirth and goes undiagnosed until a community advocate connects her with a hospital, where she is finally treated for a fistula. Her story shows that even reaching medical care is a struggle when resources are scarce and doctors are few. Heela's story shifts to gender inequality, she is raped by an older man, becomes pregnant, and is forced to choose between the pregnancy and her education, while her attacker faces no consequences at all. Together, these two stories show how women bear the full weight of circumstances they did not create.


The final two stories deepen the novel's critique by adding social and institutional dimensions. Kadiatu loses five of her six children and faces community shame, with her neighbors accusing her of occult practices — a distrust rooted in trauma from the Ebola epidemic. Her eventual C-section represents a hard-won balance between tradition and modern medicine. Tetteh's story closes the novel on its most sobering note: imprisoned for killing her abuser, she is punished by the very system that should have protected her. Placing her story last feels intentional, by this point, the reader understands that all four women are victims of the same interconnected failures, and that meaningful change requires addressing not just healthcare, but justice and equality as a whole.
